\begin{center}
{\Large\bfseries Zenith: learning what to investigate before forecasting}\\[4pt]
Will Cook \quad\textbullet\quad 12 October 2026
\end{center}
\hypertarget{idea}{}
\textbf{Abstract.}
Zenith treats reasoning as a policy for choosing useful numerical investigations.
An initial forecast may miss a distinction between sources, feature families or
times. A language-model programme proposes a small set of questions, numerical
tools answer them using earlier data, and a final decision may revise the
forecast. Later errors guide one reusable instruction change, evaluated on
separate dates. We implement this loop for six research baskets sharing the
histories of 147 financial instruments and 17 market indices. Comparisons include
an all-universe numerical model, fixed investigations and a single controller
with the same data and tools. The study is a small retrospective execution test;
its question is whether selective investigation earns its additional computation.

\textbf{1. The task.}
At each cutoff, predict the five-market-session return of every basket using the
entire declared universe. A basket's own members are only one possible source of
information: breadth in another group, lagged returns or changing volume may be
worth testing. Such patterns are empirical hypotheses. Neither correlated prices
nor a dependency between computations identifies an economic cause.

The source is the adjusted-instrument and index history published by SocrateAI
under a dataset-specific MIT-form licence~\cite{nepse}. We pin its repository
commit and import through 31 December 2025. The audited panel contains 447,243
instrument observations and 59,319 index observations; 2,319 rows with missing
index prices are quarantined. Optional blank volume values remain missing.

Membership is fixed on 29 December 2021, using only preceding observations:
at least 95\% quote coverage over 252 sessions, a quote at formation and positive
median volume. We sort the 147 eligible instruments by their preceding
126-session beta to the equal-weight universe and form six balanced groups.
These are constructed research baskets, not historical sectors or traded ETFs.
Each begins at 100 with equal initial value in its members and keeps fixed shares.
Missing quotes retain the last observed price, with quote ages and stale fractions
reported. For basket index $V_b$, the target is
\[
  r_{b,t}=V_b(t+5)/V_b(t)-1,
\]
where the offset counts available market sessions. No future-survival screen or
future price fill determines membership or the target.

\hypertarget{lab}{}
\textbf{2. Investigations produce candidates, not stories.}
A deterministic store exposes returns, volatility, volume, quote age, basket
breadth and dispersion, and market-index returns. The common provisional forecast
is a ridge regression over all features, fitted on at most 252 earlier origins
whose labels have matured. Training-only scaling, clipping and a fixed penalty
are shared by the baseline and candidate fits. Arithmetic stays in numerical code.

Six first-pass readers inspect different slices of the same universe. A planner
combines their observations and freezes an investigation plan (CP1). A query
chooses a target basket, any source basket or the whole universe or index group,
a feature family and a lag of 0, 1, 5 or 20 sessions. Up to six queries are allowed.
The tool fits the selected feature subset and reports a candidate forecast plus
its gain over the common baseline on eight earlier rolling validation origins.
Every validation fit excludes labels maturing after that validation cutoff.
\newpage

\textbf{Revision within a forecast.}
A final model selects one eligible candidate or the baseline for each basket.
The complete vector is frozen at CP2. CP1 remains an immutable record, but its
hypotheses need not survive investigation. Uninvestigated targets retain their
baseline. The tool validates candidate identity and target coverage; the language
model cannot supply an unsupported numerical forecast. A validation gain may
reflect selection noise, so retaining the baseline is a legitimate decision.

\hypertarget{oracle}{}
\textbf{3. Comparison and learning.}
Four development dates in 2023 compare directed investigation with an undirected
control. The latter runs the same reader/planner architecture but substitutes
six predeclared own-basket trend queries. Both have the same maximum query
allowance. Their first-pass model calls are independent, so the pilot does not
isolate all sampling variability.

\hypertarget{trace}{}
Evolve reads development losses and saved traces, then proposes one addition of
at most 512 characters to the planner instruction. The revision is hashed and
frozen before held-out execution. This learns a procedural instruction;
it does not change model weights, fit a neural controller or identify market
causality. One proposal is tested, including the possibility of no change.

Six later dates in 2024 compare the incumbent, its revision, the undirected arm
and a serial controller. The serial controller receives all first-pass numerical
rows and the same query catalogue. It chooses investigations and then selects
forecasts in two calls, versus eight for each graph arm. The same inexpensive
base model is used throughout. Numerical ridge and zero-return baselines require
no language-model calls. A serial programme could execute the same computation
graph; exclusive expressive capability is not the claim.

Every arm commits all six forecasts before the evaluator opens that date's
outcome. Squared fractional return error is averaged equally over targets and
origins. A failed run retains the entire numerical baseline vector, stays in the
score and is separately reported. The revision is retained only if it lowers
mean held-out loss, does not increase worst-origin loss and no model arm fails.
A shared cap of \$2 and 260 attempted calls bounds the whole experiment.

\hypertarget{results}{}
\textbf{Measured result.}
The comparison completed 221 calls to Ling 3.0 Flash for a reported
\$0.021791. Directed, revised and serial held-out forecasts equalled the ridge
baseline: mean squared return error 0.00065565, versus 0.00065196 for undirected
investigations and 0.00064589 for zero return. Eight of 32 model arms failed
candidate-to-target validation and retained the predeclared baseline fallback.
The proposed instruction was not retained. There is no observed advantage for
the directed graph or revision in this pilot. The complete forecast and failure
records are published in \path{examples/baskets/}; the proposed diagnosis remains
unverified model output.

\hypertarget{research}{}
\textbf{4. What this experiment can establish.}
A completed run demonstrates a reproducible sequence from historical data through
provisional reasoning, targeted computation, forecast revision, later scoring
and one tested instruction change. A lower observed loss would be a descriptive
result on these dates. Six held-out dates are not 36 independent experiments:
baskets share constituents' market environment and errors within a date are
dependent. The present sample does not support a claim of statistical superiority.

The open financial system need not be fully observed for forecasting to be
scored. It does, however, limit explanations. Prices and volume alone omit many
possible causes. The published data have unknown historical release timestamps
and adjustment vintages; stale marks are not executable prices; instrument types
and historical classifications are incomplete. Consequently the target is a
retrospective last-quote research index, not trading returns or a certified
point-in-time backtest. Pretrained models may also have encountered the historical
period. A prospective test is needed to address that limitation.

The architecture must survive stronger comparisons. An adaptive serial model can
read the same data and ask the same questions; a numerical model can summarise
many histories cheaply. Zenith is useful only if its investigation policy adds
value at an acceptable cost. Larger chronological tests should include repeated
unchanged controls, broader numerical baselines, cost-matched alternatives and
predeclared uncertainty estimates at the forecast-date level. Any procedure
selected after inspecting the current test results belongs to development in
its next evaluation.

\textbf{Related work and scope.}
DSPy composes language-model modules and optimises programmes against a
metric~\cite{dspy}. MIPRO optimises instructions and demonstrations across
multi-stage programmes~\cite{mipro}. Zenith applies the same general principle
of measured programme revision to a bounded forecasting investigation policy.
The specific contribution of this prototype is the executable comparison:
all-source access, numerical candidate generation, immutable provisional and
final records, full-target scoring, an inexpensive serial competitor, and a
separate test of one proposed instruction. Prompt revision alone is not novel,
and the structure of the workflow does not establish predictive benefit.

\textbf{Reproduction and evidence.}
The backend uses the existing native graph engine and its budgeted OpenRouter
adapter. Source files, import outputs, numerical snapshots, forecast commitments
and policy revisions carry hashes. Failed imports and paid attempts are retained;
no failed forecast is silently removed or replaced by a fresh attempt. The
numerical store is separate from the bounded text shown to readers. The query
catalogue, ridge specification, exact dates and data qualifications are documented
in \path{docs/baskets.md}; \path{docs/status.md} owns current results.

The previous invented System A example remains a synthetic protocol fixture.
Its preparation receipt is not evidence for this financial experiment.
Source and reproduction commands are in the
\href{https://github.com/wcook04/plectis-zenith}{Zenith repository}.
